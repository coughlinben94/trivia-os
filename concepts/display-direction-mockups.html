Five ways to keep the screen alive
after the ambient background

The brief: the ambient system is on screen ~100% of the night but reads as wallpaper. Round journeys are the opposite problem — great, and on screen ~1%. Neither is carrying the thing that actually matters, which is the question slide itself. Last year that was black text on white PowerPoint. This year it's the whole game.

The pivot in all five: stop designing what's behind the question and start designing the question. Broadcast solved this decades ago — CBS once swapped an entire graphics package and kept only the scorebug, and viewers said nothing had changed. The persistent furniture is the identity, not the scenery.

On "recognizable." The Mario instinct is right and the mechanism is wrong. At 12 feet in a dim room a guest only receives gist — layout, palette, contrast, texture. That channel carries "this is an old NES game" perfectly using entirely original art. It does not require, and legally cannot survive, the actual property. Direction 2 is built on this.

1

The Cabinet

Furniture, not wallpaper

The screen is a physical object, and it never leaves. A built frame with real material — brass, wood, glass — holds a live chip, a score bug, and one card that swaps. The background stops competing and becomes a surface. This is the scorebug lesson taken literally: build the one thing that's on screen every second, and make that the show's signature.

2

Era Decks

The legal answer to "Mario"

Recognition comes from three cues: typeface, palette, medium artifact. That's it. Chunky pixel type + a hardware-restricted palette + scanlines reads as "old NES game" instantly, with zero borrowed art. Each deck restyles the entire screen — type, color, texture, motion register — so a round change is a genuine scene change, not a hue shift. Six decks covers a season. Every one of them is 100% yours.

3

Lighting States

Cheapest, highest leverage

Delete the scene entirely. The room's mood is driven by what's happening in the game — one attribute on the root, a handful of semantic color tokens, everything downstream subscribes. Question open is cool and wide. Final ten seconds closes in amber. Lock-in drops out. A correct answer floods the room green. This is stage lighting, and it's the only direction where the visuals are functional — a guest who glances for 1.5 seconds learns the game state from peripheral color alone, without reading a word.

Habituation

Risk

On its own it's plain. This is a layer under 1, 2 or 5 — not a whole answer.

4

The Board

Jeopardy model

The identity is one persistent game object that gets marked up as the night goes. A round is a grid; every question is a cell; the cell expands into the question and collapses back with a check on it. Guests can see, without being told, how far in they are and how much is left. Jeopardy has run on exactly this for forty years — the board is the show's logo. It also quietly solves a real operational problem: "how many more of these are there?"

5

Typographic Stage

Most premium, least work

No scene, no frame, no board. Enormous type on near-flat, grain-textured color, with the question number ghosted behind at 60% of screen height. All the visual interest lives in scale, arrival and restraint. Fox stripped every ornament off their NFL bug and let a custom typeface carry the whole brand — the creative director's words were "that absence of decoration really made it seem useful." This is also the only direction that is impossible to mistake for AI output, because there's nothing to over-render.

What I'd actually do

These aren't five options to pick one from. Three of them stack, and the stack is the recommendation:

  1. Direction 3 (Lighting States) as the foundation, always on. It's the cheapest thing here and the only one that's functional rather than decorative. It survives habituation because it's tied to events, not to a loop. Build this first regardless of what else wins.
  2. Direction 5 (Typographic Stage) as the default question slide. This is the make-or-break surface and it should be the most legible thing in the building. It also makes the shiny/media questions hit harder by contrast — when a full-bleed image finally appears, it's an event.
  3. Direction 2 (Era Decks) as the per-round skin, ~6 of them. This is the direct answer to what you actually wanted at 2am. It delivers the recognition hit legally, and one deck per round means the room changes character four or five times a night instead of once.

Direction 1 is the strongest single-choice answer if you want one change instead of three — it's the safest bet and the least disruptive to the existing renderer. Direction 4 is the most differentiated but it's the biggest build and the one closest to your own anti-references list.

On the 21 ambient themes: nothing here requires deleting them. Twelve are already just the breathing gradient, which is exactly what a Lighting-States base layer wants to be. The nine bespoke ones become special-occasion decks. You're not throwing work away — you're demoting it from "the show" to "one deck in the rack."

The one hard line. A guest should be able to name the era, format or genre — never the property. Redrawing it yourself makes it a derivative work, not an original one; the 1989 Disney daycare-mural case is the exact fact pattern, and Netflix shut down an unlicensed Stranger Things bar in six weeks. The realistic downside isn't a lawsuit, it's a cease-and-desist that forces a mid-season rebuild. Cost of staying clean here is roughly zero, so there's no reason to take the risk. No character silhouettes, no signature props (question block, TARDIS, Poké Ball), no named locations, no logotypes, no recognizable theme music.
